Section 1Know your stablecoins

What's a stablecoin? A stablecoin is a digital token built to hold a steady value, usually $1. It moves on a blockchain like any cryptocurrency, so a transfer can be confirmed in seconds to minutes, any day of the week, anywhere in the world. But it's priced in dollars, so a business can use it for invoices, payroll and contracts that are written in dollars.

Three different "done" moments: (1) the network confirms the transfer, (2) it becomes final, meaning the network won't reverse it, and (3) the recipient turns the coins into money they can spend locally, which still depends on an exchange or bank at their end. The first two can take seconds. The third can take longer.

Why companies care: a regular cross-border wire passes through one to three banks (called correspondent banks). Each one charges a fee and adds a day or more. A stablecoin payment replaces that chain with one update to a shared record. The trade-off is that the risk moves from the banks in the middle to the coin itself: what backs it, who controls it, and what happens under stress.

"Stablecoin" is a family, not one thing. They all aim for a steady price, but they get there in very different ways, and each way has its own strengths and its own weak points. A finance professional needs to know which kind they're holding.

Under the hood: what a token actually is

  • A blockchain is a shared record of who owns what. Thousands of computers keep identical copies and agree on every change, so no single company can quietly edit it.
  • A token is not a file on your computer. It's a line in that shared record: "address 0x7a3… holds 250,000 coins." Sending a token updates two lines.
  • An address works like an account number. Whoever holds the matching private key can send from it. A private key is a very large secret number used to digitally sign each transaction. Unlike a password, it can't be reset: if it's lost, so are the funds, and if it's stolen, the thief controls them.
  • A smart contract is a small program stored on the blockchain that holds the rules for a token: who can create new coins (mint), who can destroy them (burn), and whether anyone can freeze an account. Everyone can read the rules, and the blockchain enforces them automatically.
  • Network fees are paid in the blockchain's own coin (ETH on Ethereum, SOL on Solana), not in the stablecoin. They're usually a fraction of a cent to a few dollars.

Why it matters to the business: the smart contract's rules decide who has power over your money. That's why two coins that both say "$1" can carry very different risks.

The six main types

Each type gets the same five questions: how it works, what's under the hood, what it does well, what can go wrong, and real examples.

A note on the labels: people use "stablecoin" loosely. Types 1, 2, 4 and 5 aim for $1. Type 3 tracks a commodity such as gold, not the dollar. In Type 6, a state-issued token like FRNT is a stablecoin, but a bank deposit token is legally a bank deposit, which is a different thing from a stablecoin even though the technology looks the same. U.S. law (Section 2F) uses a narrower term, "payment stablecoin," that covers only some of these.

1. Fiat-backed (backed by dollars held by a company)

How it works
A company (the issuer) holds $1 in cash or short-term U.S. Treasury bills for every coin. When a customer wires in dollars, the issuer mints new coins. When a customer cashes out (redeems), the issuer burns the coins and wires the dollars back.
Under the hood
The reserves sit in banks and with custodians, off the blockchain, so the blockchain can't prove they exist. Independent accounting firms check them and publish reports. The issuer holds an admin key in the smart contract that can mint, burn and freeze.
What it does well
Simple for a finance team to understand. The most widely used and easiest to trade. Already used by major payment companies: since December 2025, Visa has let some U.S. banks settle with it in USDC on the Solana blockchain. Now covered by U.S. federal law (the GENIUS Act, see Section 2).
What can go wrong
You depend on the issuer and on the banks holding its reserves. The issuer can freeze accounts. In March 2023, about $3.3 billion of USDC's reserves were stuck at Silicon Valley Bank when it failed. USDC traded down to about 88 cents for a weekend, then recovered once U.S. regulators guaranteed the bank's deposits.
Examples
USDC (Circle), USDT (Tether), PYUSD (issued by Paxos for PayPal)

2. Crypto-backed (backed by other crypto, locked in a smart contract)

How it works
A user locks crypto worth more than the coins they want, for example $150 of ETH to mint 100 coins. This is called over-collateralization. If the ETH price falls too far, the system automatically sells the locked ETH (a liquidation) to cover the coins. The extra cushion is what keeps every coin backed.
Under the hood
Smart contracts called vaults hold the collateral. Oracles (price feeds) tell the contract what the collateral is worth. Automated programs called keepers watch every vault and trigger liquidations. Holders of a governance token vote on settings such as fees and the minimum collateral ratio. Because code, not a company, runs the peg, some people call DAI "algorithmic." The difference from Type 4 is that real assets sit behind every coin.
What it does well
No single company holds the reserves. Anyone can check every vault on the blockchain at any moment. Runs 24/7 with no bank in the middle. DAI has stayed close to $1 since 2017, through several major crypto crashes.
What can go wrong
Collateral prices can fall fast. The system depends on its code, its oracles and its voters. In the March 2020 crash, the blockchain was so congested that some liquidations sold collateral for almost nothing, and the system had to raise money to cover the gap. DAI later added fiat-backed coins like USDC as collateral, which made it steadier but also tied it partly to Type 1.
Examples
DAI and its newer version USDS (Sky, formerly MakerDAO), LUSD (Liquity)

3. Commodity-backed (backed by a physical asset like gold)

How it works
Each token represents a set amount of a commodity held in a vault. For example, one PAX Gold token represents one troy ounce of gold in London vaults. Note that it's "stable" against gold, not against the dollar, so its dollar price moves with the price of gold.
Under the hood
The issuer mints a token when gold is added to the vault and burns it when gold is taken out. Bar serial numbers and vault audits are published so holders can check that the gold exists.
What it does well
You can own small fractions of gold, move them around the clock, and avoid storage and shipping costs.
What can go wrong
The price moves with gold, so it isn't a dollar payment tool. You trust the issuer and the vault, and your legal claim to the actual gold depends on the issuer's terms.
Examples
PAX Gold (PAXG), Tether Gold (XAUT)

4. Algorithmic (no reserves; code manages the supply)

How it works
There's no pile of reserves. Code grows or shrinks the supply to push the price toward $1. TerraUSD (UST) did this with a sister token, LUNA: you could always swap 1 UST for $1 worth of newly created LUNA, and the reverse. Other designs, like Ampleforth, change the number of coins in every wallet each day (a rebase).
Under the hood
The whole system lives in smart contracts. The mint and burn rules are the only thing holding the price.
What it does well
Very capital efficient: a dollar-priced coin without parking billions of dollars in a bank. Fully on-chain, with no company or bank needed.
What can go wrong
It works only while people believe the sister token has value. In May 2022, UST lost its peg, the swaps created huge amounts of new LUNA, and about $40 billion in value disappeared in under a week. The GENIUS Act does not count these as "payment stablecoins"; it orders a Treasury study of them instead.
Examples
TerraUSD (UST, collapsed in 2022), Ampleforth (AMPL)

5. Hybrid and synthetic (part backing, or a "dollar" built from hedged positions)

How it works
Hybrids mix collateral with an algorithm. Frax launched in 2020 partly backed and partly algorithmic, then its holders voted in 2023 to move to full backing. A synthetic dollar holds crypto plus an equal bet that the crypto's price will fall (a short futures position). When the crypto goes up or down, the two sides cancel out, leaving a steady dollar value.
Under the hood
Smart contracts on the blockchain, plus positions held on crypto exchanges. The short position earns or pays a funding rate that changes with the market.
What it does well
Can earn income from funding rates and staking, which can be passed on to holders. Doesn't need a bank.
What can go wrong
Funding rates can turn negative, so the coin starts paying instead of earning. It depends on exchanges and custodians. Because these coins aim to earn a return, they generally fall outside the GENIUS Act's rules for payment stablecoins.
Examples
Frax (FRAX), Ethena (USDe)

6. Government and bank-issued

How it works
A government or a bank issues the token. Wyoming launched the Frontier Stable Token (FRNT) in August 2025, backed by cash and short-term Treasuries with at least 102% in reserves. Banks are testing deposit tokens: a token that is a claim on a bank deposit, like a digital version of money in your bank account. JPMorgan began testing one, JPMD, in 2025.
Under the hood
The technology is the same as the other types. FRNT launched on seven blockchains, including Ethereum, Solana, Avalanche and Polygon. What's different is who stands behind it and which law applies.
What it does well
A well-known, regulated issuer. Wyoming's pilot cut a 45-day contractor payment cycle to near-instant settlement.
What can go wrong
Smaller and harder to trade than USDC or USDT. Often limited to certain customers or networks. Redemption rights depend on state law or the bank's terms, so they differ from coin to coin.
Examples
FRNT (State of Wyoming), JPMD (JPMorgan deposit token)

Sources: Visa (Dec. 16, 2025); The Block on FRNT (Aug. 2025); Stein Smith (2024), Ch. 5.

Section 2Design your own stablecoin

The idea: Maple's CFO is thinking about launching MapleUSD, a fiat-backed coin that Maple's dealers would use to pay their invoices. PayPal did something similar with PYUSD. In this section you make the design decisions, work out the issuer's math, read the smart contract, and check the rules.

The issuer's business model: customers hand the issuer $1 and get 1 coin. The issuer invests that dollar, mostly in short-term Treasury bills, and keeps the interest. Holders get none of it. That's how issuers make money, and it's also why reserve quality matters: an issuer chasing more interest might hold riskier assets.

2B. Is MapleUSD fully backed?

What's happening: the coverage ratio compares the reserves to the coins outstanding. 100% means exactly $1 behind every coin. Above 100% means a cushion (a surplus); below 100% means a shortfall. Under the GENIUS Act, a payment stablecoin must be backed at least 1 to 1.
ExampleCoins outstanding: $50,000,000. Reserves: $50,750,000.
Coverage ratio = 50,750,000 ÷ 50,000,000 × 100 = 101.50%. Surplus = 50,750,000 − 50,000,000 = $750,000.

2C. How the issuer makes money

ExampleCoins outstanding: $50,000,000. 15% of reserves held as cash (earns nothing), the other 85% in T-bills earning 4.00% a year.
Annual interest = 50,000,000 × 0.85 × 0.04 = $1,700,000. Per month = 1,700,000 ÷ 12 = $141,667.

2D. A wave of redemptions

What's happening: cash can be paid out right away. Assets that earn interest, like T-bills, have to be sold or borrowed against first. More cash means faster, more certain redemptions but less interest income. That's the issuer's central trade-off.
Lab simplification: to keep the math simple, this section assumes cash is available today and T-bill money arrives the next business day. In practice T-bills are among the most liquid assets in the world, and issuers can often raise cash the same day by selling them or borrowing against them (a repo). The point is the trade-off between speed, certainty and income, not that T-bills are hard to sell.
ExampleCoins outstanding: $50,000,000. Cash share: 15%. Holders ask to redeem 20% of all coins in one day.
Requests = 50,000,000 × 0.20 = $10,000,000. Cash on hand = 50,000,000 × 0.15 = $7,500,000, so that's paid today. Waiting until tomorrow = 10,000,000 − 7,500,000 = $2,500,000. To pay everyone today, cash would need to be at least 20% of reserves.

2F. Check the rules

The GENIUS Act became law on July 18, 2025. It's the first U.S. federal law for "payment stablecoins." The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) finalized its rules for the law on October 21, 2026, and the law takes effect on January 18, 2027. Key points:

  • Reserves 1 to 1, only in cash, bank deposits, Treasury bills maturing in 93 days or less, certain repurchase agreements, and government money market funds. No crypto, no corporate bonds.
  • Monthly reports of what's in the reserves, published on the issuer's website, examined by a registered public accounting firm, and certified by the CEO and CFO.
  • No interest may be paid to holders just for holding the coin.
  • Reserves can't be lent out or pledged, with a few narrow exceptions.
  • Who can issue: subsidiaries of insured banks, issuers approved by the OCC, and state-approved issuers (which move to federal oversight above $10 billion). A public company that isn't mainly in the financial business needs a unanimous vote of the Stablecoin Certification Review Committee.
  • If an issuer fails, coin holders are paid from the reserves ahead of other creditors.

Sources: Gibson Dunn, "The GENIUS Act" (July 2025); OCC News Release 2026-92 (Oct. 21, 2026).

Section 3Pay with it

The idea: Maple doesn't have to issue its own coin to use one. Here you compare paying a supplier by wire and by stablecoin, read the payment on a block explorer, split a paycheck automatically, and net out payments between Maple's own offices.

3A. Wire or stablecoin?

What's happening: a wire to Mexico usually costs three things: the sending bank's fee, a fee from each correspondent bank in the middle, and an FX spread, the hidden markup in the exchange rate. The spread is a percentage of the payment, so it's often the biggest cost.

A stablecoin payment also has costs: a small fee to convert dollars into the coin, the network fee, and the supplier's cost to turn the coin into pesos at a local exchange (the off-ramp spread). Fiat is only converted at the two ends; the middle is one transfer.

Lab assumptions: Aceros can receive USDC and convert it to pesos through a licensed provider, and the fees below are the only costs. Real comparisons are rarely that clean. You'll name what this model leaves out in the written question at the end of Section 3.

ExampleInvoice: $200,000. Wire: $40 bank fee, 1 correspondent bank charging $20, FX spread 1.50%. Stablecoin: 0.10% conversion fee, $0.25 network fee, 0.40% off-ramp spread.
Wire cost = 40 + 20 × 1 + 200,000 × 0.015 = 40 + 20 + 3,000 = $3,060.00
Stablecoin cost = 200,000 × 0.001 + 0.25 + 200,000 × 0.004 = 200 + 0.25 + 800 = $1,000.25
Savings = 3,060.00 − 1,000.25 = $2,059.75, which is 2,059.75 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = 1.03% of the invoice.
Early-payment discount: if the supplier gives 1.0% off for paying within 24 hours, that's worth 200,000 × 0.01 = $2,000.00.

3B. Under the hood: read the block explorer

A block explorer is a public website that shows every transaction on a blockchain. Anyone can look up a payment by its transaction hash, a unique ID made by running the transaction's details through a hash function (the same kind of math as SHA-256).

Why it matters to the business: both sides can see that the payment happened, when and for how much, without calling a bank. Once a payment is finalized, the network won't reverse it: no batch cutoff and no waiting for a bank to confirm. Two things finality does not mean: a fiat-backed issuer can still freeze the coins at the receiving address afterward, and a payment sent by mistake can still be recovered off the blockchain, through the contract with the supplier or in court. The payment is also public. Anyone who knows Maple's address can see its payments, which is a privacy issue for a company.

Network fees are paid in the blockchain's own coin. To get the fee in dollars, multiply the fee in that coin by that coin's price.

ExampleNetwork fee: 0.000150 ETH. ETH price: $3,000. Fee in dollars = 0.000150 × 3,000 = $0.4500.

3C. Payroll that splits itself

Under the hood: a smart contract can split one payment into pieces at the moment it's paid. For payroll, it could send the net pay to the worker's address and the tax withholding to a tax account in the same transaction. Today, companies hold withholdings and send them on a schedule later. This is a hypothetical: U.S. tax authorities don't currently accept payroll taxes in stablecoins, so treat this as a picture of what programmable payroll could do.
ExampleGross pay: $5,000. Withholding rate: 20%. Withholding = 5,000 × 0.20 = $1,000.00. Net pay = 5,000 − 1,000 = $4,000.00.

3D. Netting between Maple's offices

What's happening: Maple's U.S., Mexico and Canada offices owe each other money. Instead of paying every bill separately, the treasury team adds up what each office is owed and what it owes, and only moves the difference. This is called netting. Each office's net position = what it's owed − what it owes. A positive number means it receives money; a negative number means it pays. All the net positions add up to zero. The money that actually moves is the total of the positive net positions.
ExampleU.S. owes Mexico $500,000. Mexico owes Canada $300,000. Canada owes U.S. $200,000.
Mexico: owed 500,000 − owes 300,000 = +$200,000. Canada: owed 300,000 − owes 200,000 = +$100,000. U.S.: owed 200,000 − owes 500,000 = −$300,000.
Instead of $1,000,000 in three payments, only $300,000 moves (200,000 + 100,000).

Section 4Stress test

The idea: every stablecoin design keeps its price near $1 through a specific mechanism. Here you work through each mechanism, both when it works and when it's under pressure. A CFO doesn't need to love or fear stablecoins, but they do need to know what holds the price up.

4A. How a fiat-backed coin holds $1: the arbitrage loop

What's happening: arbitrage means buying something in one place and selling it in another for a profit.

  • If the coin trades below $1, a trader buys coins cheaply on an exchange and redeems them with the issuer for $1 each. The issuer burns those coins, the supply falls, and the price rises back toward $1.
  • If the coin trades above $1, a trader gives the issuer $1 to mint a new coin and sells it on an exchange for more. New supply pushes the price back down.

The peg is held by this loop, not by faith in the brand. And the loop only works if redemption at $1 is actually available when traders need it.

ExamplePrice: $0.980. A trader buys 100,000 coins and redeems them. The issuer charges a 0.05% redemption fee.
Profit = 100,000 × (1 − 0.980) − 100,000 × 0.0005 = 2,000 − 50 = $1,950.00.
Price: $1.015. A trader mints 100,000 coins at $1 and sells them. Profit = 100,000 × (1.015 − 1) = $1,500.00.

4B. When redemptions stop

What's happening: this is what happened to USDC over the weekend of March 10, 2023. Part of the reserves were stuck at a failed bank and the banking system was closed for the weekend, so redemptions slowed. With the loop broken, the price fell. Most of the reserves were fine the whole time. Once regulators guaranteed the bank's deposits, redemptions resumed and the price returned to $1.
ExampleCoins outstanding: $40,000,000,000. 8% of reserves at the failed bank: 40,000,000,000 × 0.08 = $3,200,000,000 stuck.
A company holds 1,000,000 coins and sells them at $0.900: it gets $900,000, a loss of 1,000,000 × (1 − 0.900) = $100,000.00.

4C. How a crypto-backed coin holds $1: vaults and liquidation

What's happening: the collateral ratio = value of the locked collateral ÷ coins minted × 100. If the minimum is 150%, the most you can mint is the collateral value ÷ 1.5. The liquidation price is the collateral price at which the ratio hits exactly 150%: coins minted × 1.5 ÷ amount of collateral. Below that price, the vault is liquidated and its collateral is sold to repay the coins.
ExampleLock 100 ETH at $3,000. Collateral value = 100 × 3,000 = $300,000. Most you can mint at 150% = 300,000 ÷ 1.5 = $200,000.
Mint 150,000 coins instead (a safer ratio of 200%). Liquidation price = 150,000 × 1.5 ÷ 100 = $2,250.00 per ETH.
If ETH falls 30%: new value = 300,000 × 0.70 = 210,000. New ratio = 210,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = 140.00%, which is below 150%, so the vault is liquidated.
Under the hood: oracles. A smart contract can't look up a price on its own. An oracle feeds it outside data, like the ETH price. Good systems combine several sources so one bad reading can't move it. But if the feed is wrong, the contract acts on the wrong number, and code doesn't stop to ask questions. Prediction markets have seen the same weakness: in one case, someone heated the weather sensor a bet relied on to win a temperature bet.

4D. How an algorithmic coin holds $1, and what happened to Terra

What's happening: with UST, anyone could burn 1 UST and receive $1 worth of newly created LUNA. When UST was below $1, this let traders profit and shrank UST's supply, much like the arbitrage loop in 4A. The difference is what you get when you redeem: a fiat-backed coin pays out dollars from a reserve, while UST paid out new LUNA. LUNA minted = dollars of UST burned ÷ LUNA price. When LUNA's price falls, each dollar of UST burned creates more LUNA.
ExampleDay 1: $500,000,000 of UST burned, LUNA at $80.00. LUNA minted = 500,000,000 ÷ 80 = 6,250,000 = 6.25 million.
Day 2: same amount burned, LUNA at $32.00. Minted = 500,000,000 ÷ 32 = 15,625,000 = 15.63 million. That's 2.5 times as much new LUNA, which pushes LUNA's price down further.
